### Topic 1: Principles of Auditing (ISO 19011:2018, Clause 4)

**A1.** This is an **independence** issue, not a competence one. The principle exists precisely because self-assessed objectivity isn't reliable evidence of actual objectivity — a recent, direct prior role in the exact process area being audited creates a structural conflict of interest regardless of the individual's honest intentions. The standard's logic is that independence should be designed into the audit assignment (who gets assigned where) rather than relied upon as a personal virtue after the fact. 14 months and a direct prior operational role in the same control area is the kind of detail exam scenarios use specifically to test whether you'll accept "but I'm sure I can be fair" as a resolution — it isn't one; the assignment itself should be reconsidered or independently reviewed.
*Reference: ISO 19011:2018, Clause 4 — Independence.* 🟢

**A2.** This is a **fair presentation** issue. Fair presentation requires that findings, conclusions, and reports reflect the audit activities truthfully and accurately — and explicitly includes reporting unresolved differences of opinion between the audit team and the auditee, rather than smoothing them away. Softening a legitimate, evidence-based finding to preserve relationship comfort is exactly what this principle exists to prevent. If the disagreement can't be resolved, the report should state the finding as evidenced, and separately record the auditee's disputing position — not blend the two into a watered-down middle ground.
*Reference: ISO 19011:2018, Clause 4 — Fair presentation.* 🟢

**A3.** This is an **evidence-based approach** failure (with a secondary due professional care angle). A single verbal assurance from one individual, with no records reviewed and no sampling performed, doesn't meet the standard of a "rational method" for reaching a verifiable conclusion — audit conclusions need to rest on evidence that is traceable and could, in principle, be checked by someone else. What should have happened: pull actual access review records, sample a reasonable cross-section of accounts, and verify the review activity actually occurred as described — time pressure is a planning/resourcing problem to flag, not a reason to lower the evidentiary bar.
*Reference: ISO 19011:2018, Clause 4 — Evidence-based approach (due professional care also relevant, since diligence should scale with what's at stake).* 🟢

**A4.** This is a **confidentiality** breach, and the justification is not valid. Confidentiality covers all information obtained or created during the audit process, not merely what ends up written in the final report — "it wasn't in the official report" is not an exemption. Sharing competitively sensitive information with a rival firm is close to a textbook example of the kind of disclosure this principle is designed to prevent, regardless of how incidentally it was learned.
*Reference: ISO 19011:2018, Clause 4 — Confidentiality.* 🟢

**A5.** **C.**
- A is wrong — nothing in the scenario suggests unauthorized record access.
- B is wrong — the time pressure here is a resourcing/planning issue, not an independence conflict (no bias or conflict of interest described).
- D is wrong — nothing indicates a communication failure to the auditee.
- C matches: the core issue is generalizing from an unjustified, arbitrarily small sample without a stated rational basis — precisely what the evidence-based principle exists to prevent.
*Reference: ISO 19011:2018, Clause 4 — Evidence-based approach.* 🟢
